Assessing Park Satisfaction through Data Integration and Correlation Analysis in R

Combined three public datasets covering Seoul’s 25 districts to test whether greater park area per capita is associated with higher satisfaction with urban green spaces. Found no meaningful relationship in any of the four years analyzed.

02Goal

Korean law sets a standard of 6 m² of urban parkland per person, and park policy is often evaluated based on the amount of parkland available in each district. However, more parkland does not necessarily mean greater resident satisfaction with green spaces. Using public data from Seoul’s 25 districts from 2021 to 2024. This project asked:

  • Which districts fall below the standard of 6 m² of urban parkland per capita?
  • Are residents more satisfied with green spaces in districts with more parkland per capita?
  • Did resident satisfaction increase in districts where parkland area grew?

03Methodology & Summary

  • Background: First planned in a 2022 course with 2021 data, then rebuilt in R for 2021–2024.
  • Data: Three public datasets from the Seoul Open Data Plaza: park area, park area per person and a resident satisfaction survey.
  • Data integration: The three tables had different layouts and multi-row headers. I wrote a reusable R function to reshape each table into a consistent format, with one row per district and year. I then joined them into a single dataset covering 25 districts over four years and verified that all districts were represented in every year.
  • Measures: Park area per capita (m²) assessed against the 6 m² standard, and the percentage of residents who were somewhat or very satisfied with green spaces.
  • Analysis: Examined the relationship between park area per person and resident satisfaction in each year, using a method less affected by extreme values in districts such as Jongno-gu. Also compared changes in park area and satisfaction from 2021 to 2024.

Spearman’s ρ (rho) shows the strength and direction of the rank-based relationship (−1 to 1; values near 0 indicate a weak relationship). The p-value indicates how compatible the observed result is with the assumption of no relationship; p < 0.05 suggests that the observed relationship is statistically significant.

  • Larger park area did not mean higher satisfaction. The relationship was weak in every year (Spearman’s ρ: −0.02 to 0.21), with no statistically significant correlation.
  • Meeting the 6 m² standard did not mean higher satisfaction. The 8 districts below the standard had nearly the same satisfaction rate as the others (46.3% vs. 45.8% in 2021).
  • The district with the most park area was not the most satisfied. Jongno-gu ranked 1st in park area every year, but ranked 21st, 13th, 23rd, and 2nd in satisfaction.(2021-2024)
  • Satisfaction increased even as park area remained nearly unchanged. From 2021 to 2024, satisfaction rose by 9.1 percentage points, while park area per person increased by only 0.21 m².

05From Analysis to Decision

  • Don't treat the 6 m² standard as the only measure of success. Park area per person was not strongly associated with resident satisfaction, so park planners should not rely on it as their main measure of success.
  • Consider what park area figures leave out. Planning should also account for how close parks are to people's homes, how easy they are to access and use, and how well they are maintained.
  • Limitations: The analysis covered only 25 districts, making weak relationships difficult to detect. District-level averages also hide differences between neighborhoods. Future research could examine smaller areas and include accessibility measures, such as walking distance to the nearest park.
